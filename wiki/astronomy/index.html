Astronomy

Stellar types, planetary geology and atmospheres, and the tradeoffs of different systems.

Stars and systems

A star sets the energy available to its worlds. Its mass, brightness, spectrum, age, and activity influence climate and atmospheric survival. System architecture then determines how far colonies must travel and which resources are nearby.

SystemOpportunitiesTradeoffs
Red dwarf / M starLong stellar lifetimes; temperate orbits close to the star can place useful worlds near one another.Close temperate planets are more susceptible to tidal locking. Stellar activity and early irradiation can threaten atmospheres and water. Compact distances do not automatically mean cheap transfers.
K starLong-lived stars with temperate orbits farther out than around dim M stars.Activity, planetary inventory, and orbital spacing still matter; stellar class alone does not guarantee a good home.
G star / Sun-likeA familiar reference for temperate rocky worlds and solar-powered industry.A spread-out system can make interplanetary logistics slow. A Sun-like star does not guarantee Earth-like planets.
Hotter, more massive starsGreater luminosity and more distant temperate zones.Shorter stellar lifetimes and stronger ultraviolet radiation can complicate long-term habitability.
Multiple starsDistinct orbital neighborhoods and changing illumination.Orbital stability and climate depend on the arrangement; not every apparent orbit is viable.

Planetary science

Geology and resources

The design separates broad geological provinces from concentrated mineral deposits. Mafic and felsic crust provide common rock resources; volcanic and hydrothermal processes can concentrate metal-bearing ores. Water history, volcanism, impacts, and weathering help explain where valuable deposits occur.

Atmospheres and climate

Pressure, composition, greenhouse effects, and the water inventory must be considered together. A planet in the temperate zone may still be airless, toxic, or covered by a dense greenhouse atmosphere. Geological and atmospheric history give colonies different industrial opportunities and environmental costs.

Tidally locked planets

A planet synchronized to its star keeps approximately the same hemisphere facing it. This can provide persistent daylight for solar power and fixed illumination zones for settlement, but also strong day–night contrasts. Atmospheric and oceanic heat transport can moderate those contrasts; a habitable twilight belt is a possibility, not a universal rule.

Design implications: choose between illuminated industrial sites, cooler settlement regions, and dark-side resources. Slow rotation gives little launch assistance and can make waiting for a launch site to align with an orbital plane expensive in time. Climate, landing access, and transport routes should all influence colony placement.

Tidally locked moons

A moon locked to its planet keeps the same face toward the planet, not necessarily toward the star. It can still have a solar day. Eclipses, tidal heating, and the parent planet’s radiation environment depend on its orbit and host.

Design implications: low-gravity moons can be attractive industrial bases with nearby destinations. Against that, compare volatile supply, surface conditions, eclipse-driven power interruptions, and the energy needed to travel through the parent planet’s gravity well.
