Terminal Burn / Field guide

Astronavigation

Orbital mechanics, transfer windows, launch planning, and rendezvous trajectories.

Orbits and delta-v

An orbit is a trajectory around a gravitating body. Changing it requires a change in velocity, measured as delta-v. Reaching a destination means matching its position and velocity at the same time; crossing its orbit alone is not a rendezvous.

Interplanetary transfers: Lambert’s solution

Terminal Burn has a Lambert solver for plotting ballistic transfers. Given a departure position, an arrival position, a time of flight, and the gravity of the central body, Lambert’s problem finds a connecting two-body trajectory and its departure and arrival velocities. The destination’s position is evaluated at arrival time, so the ship aims for where the target will be.

Comparing these transfer velocities with the departure and destination orbital velocities gives the velocity changes needed to leave and rendezvous. Local escape and capture must also be included: a low-cost cruise arc does not necessarily mean a low-cost complete journey. Different departure dates and flight times offer different tradeoffs between waiting, travel time, and delta-v.

A Hohmann-style transfer remains a useful reference for ideal circular, coplanar orbits, but the planner is not restricted to that geometry. Lambert transfers can connect eccentric or inclined orbits and support higher-energy fast transfers. A Lambert solution describes an unpowered coast between endpoints; sustained-thrust flight is a different trajectory model.

Hierarchical escape and capture

The planner combines Lambert transfers with patched-conic escape and capture across the orbital hierarchy. Each segment is modeled relative to its governing body, with the ship’s position and velocity expressed in the appropriate frame as the route moves between local and parent systems.

This allows a destination to be nested several levels deep. For example, a ship can plot a transfer to a moon orbiting a planet around a binary companion star. A journey from another planetary system within the binary can include:

  1. Escape from the departure planet and, where required, its host star’s local system.
  2. Transfer across the binary’s shared orbital hierarchy toward the companion star.
  3. Enter the companion’s system and approach the target planet.
  4. Continue into the moon’s local environment and capture into the intended destination orbit.

The route must account for the motion of the companion star, planet, and moon, together with the escape and capture requirements of the connected segments. Reaching the companion star is not the same as rendezvousing with its planet or moon.

Patched conics make these routes practical to plan within the game’s hierarchical two-body model. They approximate the gravitational environment rather than solving the simultaneous influence of every body; this distinction is especially relevant in binary systems.

Transfer windows

A launch window reflects both alignment and the transfer cost the ship can accept. For ideal circular orbits, the synodic period gives the repetition interval of relative orbital phase. General Lambert transfers depend on the actual departure and arrival geometry; a single ideal phase angle does not describe every eccentric, inclined, or hierarchical journey.

Constant-thrust trajectories

Military ships can trade propellant for time. In the intended design, delta-v budgets of a few thousand km/s allow fast transits using sustained thrust rather than long, mostly ballistic transfer orbits. Acceleration, available delta-v, departure geometry, and arrival braking all constrain the journey.

A simple fast-transit model accelerates toward the destination, turns the ship, and brakes to match the destination’s motion. If the delta-v budget is insufficient to thrust throughout, the ship uses an accelerate–coast–decelerate profile. The midpoint turn is only symmetric when acceleration and endpoint conditions permit it; constant thrust also does not imply constant acceleration as propellant is consumed.

The same delta-v budget must cover acceleration and braking. Spending it all outbound produces a fast flyby rather than a rendezvous. Military ships can depart outside economical transfer windows, but geometry still affects cost and time.

Interstellar arrival: braking from 0.1c

The starting colony ship arrives at roughly 0.1c, about 30,000 km/s, and must shed nearly all that incoming speed relative to the destination system before it can establish a useful bound orbit. The braking burn begins far outside the planetary system; it cannot be postponed until the ship reaches its intended colony world.

As a scale estimate, braking from 0.1c at a constant 1g takes about 35 days and begins roughly 300 AU before the stopping point. At 0.1g it takes about 354 days and roughly 3,000 AU. These are straight-line, constant-acceleration estimates that neglect stellar gravity and use a low-speed approximation; flight planning must account for changing mass, thrust capability, propellant, and the desired final orbit.

This interstellar braking requirement is far larger than the few-thousand-km/s budget of an in-system military ship. After slowing, the colony ship still needs to target the system correctly, enter its intended orbit, and reserve maneuvering capability for local operations.

Bulk haulers and distant ice expeditions

Bulk haulers prioritize delivered cargo and propellant economy. They normally use transfer orbits with departure and arrival burns separated by a long coast. For distant ice-mining expeditions, a modified fast transfer can spend additional delta-v to shorten the voyage without sustaining thrust throughout.

Faster transfers also need more capable arrival braking. Expedition planning must include the outward leg, matching the mining target, and returning with a heavier cargo. A large ice deposit is only useful if the hauler can bring material home at an acceptable transport cost and turnaround time.

Surface to station

  1. Plane alignment: launch when the site passes through the station’s orbital plane, or budget for a different ascent and plane change.
  2. Orbit insertion: reach a suitable parking or transfer orbit.
  3. Phasing: use a different orbital period to let the ship and station reach the encounter point together.
  4. Rendezvous: match velocity before final approach and docking.

A direct ascent may avoid waiting in a parking orbit but requires favorable geometry. A vehicle capable of relocating before ascent can trade atmospheric flight against orbital waiting and maneuver costs.

Slow rotation and tidal locking

On a slowly rotating world, a fixed launch site can wait a long time for plane alignment. Depending on vehicle capability, alternatives include relocating to another launch site, accepting a plane change, or entering a parking orbit and waiting. A moon locked to its planet needs its own rotation and orbital geometry considered.

Trajectory tradeoffs

Compare propellant, travel time, departure delay, and arrival conditions. Higher-energy transfers may arrive sooner but demand more departure or braking capability. Continuous-thrust trajectories also depend on thrust and burn duration, so an impulsive transfer estimate is only a baseline.

Ship roles and propulsion budgets on this page describe the intended Terminal Burn design. They do not certify that every trajectory option is available in the current build.
