Astronavigation

Orbital mechanics, transfer windows, launch planning, and rendezvous trajectories.

Orbits and delta-v

An orbit is a trajectory around a gravitating body. Changing it requires a change in velocity, measured as delta-v. Reaching a destination means matching its position and velocity at the same time; crossing its orbit alone is not a rendezvous.

Interplanetary transfers

A Hohmann-style transfer between ideal circular, coplanar orbits follows half an ellipse whose apsides touch the two orbital radii. It provides a simple baseline for travel time and departure alignment. Real eccentricity, inclination, and propulsion limits can change the result.

The target must be in the right place when the ship arrives. A launch window is therefore about future alignment. The synodic period gives the repetition interval of relative orbital phase for ideal circular orbits; it does not reproduce every detail of an eccentric or inclined encounter.

Surface to station

  1. Plane alignment: launch when the site passes through the station’s orbital plane, or budget for a different ascent and plane change.
  2. Orbit insertion: reach a suitable parking or transfer orbit.
  3. Phasing: use a different orbital period to let the ship and station reach the encounter point together.
  4. Rendezvous: match velocity before final approach and docking.

A direct ascent may avoid waiting in a parking orbit but requires favorable geometry. A vehicle capable of relocating before ascent can trade atmospheric flight against orbital waiting and maneuver costs.

Slow rotation and tidal locking

On a slowly rotating world, a fixed launch site can wait a long time for plane alignment. Depending on vehicle capability, alternatives include relocating to another launch site, accepting a plane change, or entering a parking orbit and waiting. A moon locked to its planet needs its own rotation and orbital geometry considered.

Trajectory tradeoffs

Compare propellant, travel time, departure delay, and arrival conditions. Higher-energy transfers may arrive sooner but demand more departure or braking capability. Continuous-thrust trajectories also depend on thrust and burn duration, so an impulsive transfer estimate is only a baseline.

These are navigation principles and planning strategies. This page does not certify that every trajectory option is available in the current build.
