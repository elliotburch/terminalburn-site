Terminal Burn / Field guide

Astronomy

Stellar types, planetary geology and atmospheres, and the tradeoffs of different systems.

Stars and systems

Stellar mass and luminosity set the scale of a system’s temperate region. The habitable zone (HZ) is a range where a suitable atmosphere could permit surface liquid water; it does not guarantee an Earth-like environment. The groups below describe the intended system tradeoffs in Terminal Burn.

M/K — low-mass, compact systems

Red M dwarfs and orange K dwarfs put their temperate worlds closer to the star than brighter stellar types. Compact planetary layouts tend to offer shorter journeys and, for comparable relative orbital spacing in the temperate region, lower transfer delta-v. Neighboring worlds can form a closely connected industrial network. The actual cost still depends on orbital spacing, inclination, and the depth of each planet’s gravity well.

Tidal locking is especially likely for close temperate planets around M stars and can affect cooler K-star systems. It is not universal across K stars: warmer K stars can host temperate planets with ordinary day–night cycles. M-star activity and early irradiation can also threaten water and atmospheres.

The logistical advantage in space can be offset at the surface. Slow rotation supplies little launch assistance, and a launch site may wait a long time for orbital-plane alignment. A planet synchronized to its star cannot support a conventional rotation-supported space elevator: its synchronous orbital radius lies outside its Hill sphere. Alternative tether concepts would be a separate technology.

A/F/G — systems with potential Earth analogs

These systems provide the broader, more widely spaced temperate neighborhoods familiar from the Solar System. G stars include the Sun; F and A stars are hotter and brighter rather than literal solar twins. Any of these groups can be considered for Earth-analog worlds in the game, subject to atmosphere, geology, and stellar history.

An A star can place its habitable belt around 5 AU, depending on luminosity. That pushes settlement and resource networks onto a larger physical scale. Comparable transfers can take longer and demand more delta-v, while longer orbital periods can make opportunities between proportionally spaced orbits less frequent. Window frequency depends on both orbits, so distance alone is not enough to determine it. Hotter stars also have shorter main-sequence lifetimes.

B/O — rare, massive stars

Massive blue stars are rare, luminous, and short-lived. If a useful temperate region exists, it can be extremely distant. A temperature-based HZ does not ensure a viable home: strong radiation, stellar winds, and rapid evolution may rule out practical surface habitability. These systems offer extreme environments and expensive logistics rather than reliable Earth analogs.

Giant stars — evolved systems

Giant stars are an evolutionary stage, not another main-sequence mass group. Increasing luminosity can move temperate conditions into formerly frozen outer regions, including belts around 30 AU in suitable systems. This is an example rather than an upper limit; the position and duration depend on the star’s evolution.

Such systems can combine thawed outer worlds with scorched inner planets, enormous travel distances, and long transfer times. They may also include chthonian planets: candidate remnants of giant planets whose envelopes were stripped away, potentially including survivors of interaction with an expanding star. This is a possible exotic planet history, not a guaranteed product of every giant-star system.

Planetary science

Geology and resources

The design separates broad geological provinces from concentrated mineral deposits. Mafic and felsic crust provide common rock resources; volcanic and hydrothermal processes can concentrate metal-bearing ores. Water history, volcanism, impacts, and weathering help explain where valuable deposits occur.

Atmospheres and climate

Pressure, composition, greenhouse effects, and the water inventory must be considered together. A planet in the temperate zone may still be airless, toxic, or covered by a dense greenhouse atmosphere. Geological and atmospheric history give colonies different industrial opportunities and environmental costs.

Tidally locked planets

A planet synchronized to its star keeps approximately the same hemisphere facing it. This can provide persistent daylight for solar power and fixed illumination zones for settlement, but also strong day–night contrasts. Atmospheric and oceanic heat transport can moderate those contrasts; a habitable twilight belt is a possibility, not a universal rule.

Design implications: choose between illuminated industrial sites, cooler settlement regions, and dark-side resources. Slow rotation gives little launch assistance and can make waiting for a launch site to align with an orbital plane expensive in time. Star-synchronous planets also lack a viable synchronous orbit for a conventional space elevator. Climate, landing access, and transport routes should all influence colony placement.

Tidally locked moons

A moon locked to its planet keeps the same face toward the planet, not necessarily toward the star. It can still have a solar day. Eclipses, tidal heating, and the parent planet’s radiation environment depend on its orbit and host.

Design implications: low-gravity moons can be attractive industrial bases with nearby destinations. Against that, compare volatile supply, surface conditions, eclipse-driven power interruptions, and the energy needed to travel through the parent planet’s gravity well.
